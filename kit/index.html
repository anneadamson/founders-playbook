Playbook for founders · Estonian OÜ

You and your friends are about to build something. Agree on the boring part first.

Who owns what, who decides, and what happens if someone leaves. Most friendships survive a bad product. Few survive a vague share split. This playbook walks you through the choices, and gives you a plain-English agreement you can fill in together.

Answer the questions and get your draftRead the short version first

Why do we need an agreement if we are friends?

You do not write it because you distrust each other. You write it because later, when money, stress or a new idea shows up, you will not remember the same conversation the same way.

What goes wrong?What does the agreement do?
A founder quits after three months and keeps a third of the company.Vesting. Shares are earned over time. Leave early and the company takes back the part you have not earned.
You disagree about taking an investor. Nobody can break the tie.Decision rules and a deadlock process. You know in advance which votes decide what, and what happens if you are stuck.
One founder works full-time. Another keeps a day job and does evenings.Roles and time. The expected hours and the allowed side work are written down.
A founder sells their shares to a stranger you have never met.First refusal and approval. You get the first chance to buy, and a stranger can only come in if the board approves.
A founder leaves and starts a copy of the business next door.Secrecy, non-compete and non-poaching. Limited in time and place so that they are fair.
Code and designs belong to whoever wrote them, not to the company.IP clause. What you build for the company belongs to the company.

How does this work?

  1. Talk first.Sit down together with the 28 questions below. Do not look for the "right" answer. Look for the answer all of you can live with.
  2. Fill in the questionnaire.The generator turns your answers into a plain-English agreement you can read on screen.
  3. Fix the company's articles.In Estonia, some rules only work if they are also in the articles of association. The agreement lists what the articles must say.
  4. Ask a lawyer to check it.A lawyer reads one document, not a blank page. That is faster and cheaper.
  5. Sign it.With digital signatures or by hand. Keep one copy each.

What do the three have in common?

  • Estonian law, and the Harju County Court as the first court.
  • Company and shareholders both sign.
  • Articles of association come as a separate document and form part of the agreement. Only the Estonian text of the articles is registered.
  • Four-year vesting with a one-year cliff as the standard, 90 days for the company to use its right to take back shares, fair value set by an expert if you disagree.
  • Non-compete and non-poaching run while you work for the company and 12 months after.
  • Secrecy lasts three years after you stop being a shareholder.

This playbook and the generator are built around the Founders' Agreement, because it is the one you need before any investor arrives. When an investor comes, you move to a shareholders' agreement. Your founders' agreement does not disappear: its vesting, leaver and non-compete rules carry over.

What does Estonian law add?

A contract between friends is not enough in an Estonian OÜ. Some rules come from the Commercial Code, and some only work if they are written in the articles of association. These are the ones that catch people out. Each rule links to the official text in the Riigi Teataja.

Which ten traps do founders fall into?

  1. Splitting the shares equally because it feels fair. Equal shares are fine if everyone does the same amount of work for the same time. Write down why you chose the split.
  2. No vesting. Without it, a founder who leaves after a month keeps a full share. Investors will ask for vesting anyway.
  3. Two founders at 50/50 with "more than half" decides. You can never decide anything without both. Pick a deadlock rule.
  4. Everyone can sign for the company. Each management board member can usually bind the company alone. Set a spending limit.
  5. Signing with a PDF and forgetting the notary. A share transfer still needs a notary unless your articles say otherwise.
  6. A "forever" non-compete. A court may not accept it. Keep it to 6 to 12 months and to where you really work.
  7. The business sentence is too vague. It decides what counts as competing. Write it carefully.
  8. Nobody owns the code. If a friend wrote the first version before the company existed, make sure it is assigned to the company in writing.
  9. Part-time founders and full-time founders on the same vesting. Talk about it now. This kit does not slow down part-time vesting for you.
  10. Treating the agreement as done once it is signed. Look at it again when you hire your first person and when an investor appears.
